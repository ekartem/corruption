Corruption and Trust Dynamics: A Model Description for Political Scientists

This document describes an agent-based model (ABM) of corruption and institutional trust. The model joins two processes that are often discussed separately: the capture of public money by corrupt officials, and the gradual erosion of citizens’ trust in public institutions.

The model is not a calibrated forecast. It does not claim to predict corruption or trust in a specific country. Its purpose is more limited and more theoretical: to test whether a given set of mechanisms is sufficient to produce a recognizable pattern, such as a low-trust corruption trap. The model is written in NetLogo.

Two research questions organize the model, with a third, subsidiary question about the mechanism that connects them.

RQ1 (trust). At what level of perceived corruption does institutional trust among ordinary citizens tip into collapse?

RQ2 (network). When transparency raises the odds of detection at selected spending channels, does the corrupt patron-client network dissolve, fragment, or relocate to more opaque channels?

Mechanism question. Does distrust mobilize civic oversight, or does it demobilize citizens and deepen the trap?

1. Theoretical premises

The model rests on a small set of theoretical choices. Each choice is tied to a source and translated into a rule inside the simulation.

First, corruption is treated not as an individual moral failure, but as a routine informal rule. This follows neo-institutional account of corruption as an informal political institution and an “institutional trap” (Chabanna, 2019). The corrupt actor in the model is boundedly rational. It does not maximize in a fully informed way. It compares the expected cost of the formal route with the expected cost of the informal one, and chooses the path that appears cheaper.

This logic fits broader definition of institutions as the “rules of the game”, including both formal laws and informal customs (North, 1990). It also follows Helmke and Levitsky’s distinction between formal and informal institutions, where corruption may compete with, substitute for, or undermine the formal rule (Helmke & Levitsky, 2004). The persistence of corruption across changes in leadership is therefore treated as a self-reproducing feature of the political system, not as a problem of individual office-holders alone (Huss, 2023).

War enters the model as an opportunity structure. Emergency procurement and reconstruction spending bring together discretion, urgency, money, and weakened oversight. This increases the rent available for capture (Klitgaard, 1988; Savchenko et al., 2018).

Institutional trust is treated as performance-based and fragile. It is the impersonal expectation that public institutions will act predictably, fairly, and according to their stated rules. In this view, institutions that work generate trust; institutions that fail generate skepticism (Mishler & Rose, 2001). Rothstein’s argument adds the trap mechanism: when institutions are seen as corrupt, even honest actors may be pushed toward corrupt behavior because they do not expect others to follow the rules (Rothstein, 2005). More broadly, the connection between corruption, inequality, and low social trust also follows Rothstein and Uslaner’s argument that generalized trust depends on institutions being experienced as fair and impartial (Rothstein & Uslaner, 2005). Sztompka’s theory of trust supplies the idea of virtuous and vicious spirals (Sztompka, 1999). Johnson and Melnikov’s “wisdom of distrust” explains why distrust can be rational and sticky in contexts where institutions have repeatedly failed (Johnson & Melnikov, 2009).

The model simplifies trust into a single value carried by each citizen. This is useful for the first version of the model, but it is also a clear limitation. It cannot reproduce the object-specific asymmetry typical of Ukraine, where the same person may strongly trust the army and strongly distrust courts or prosecutors (Izha et al., 2020).

Rent capture is organized through a patron-client network. The model assumes a two-layer structure: formal institutions operate on the surface, while informal ties allocate real resources underneath. This reflects the neopatrimonial “sistema” described for Ukraine (Fisun, 2016). A small number of oligarch-like hubs anchor the network (Karnowski & Szufel, 2025). Civil watchdogs are modeled as a separate actor type because anti-corruption activists and organizations often monitor and expose corruption precisely where state bodies are not trusted to act (Bader et al., 2019).

Finally, trust collapse is modeled as a threshold process. Trust does not necessarily decline smoothly. It may hold under pressure, then collapse once a tolerance level is crossed. The model represents this through a threshold cascade: local shifts into distrust can become system-wide when vulnerable citizens form a large enough connected cluster (Watts, 2002).

The model defines link between distrust and civic participation as a mobilization mechanism for mobilize citizens into vigilance (Johnson & Melnikov, 2009).

2. Social mechanisms implemented

The model implements twelve mechanisms. Together, they connect corrupt capture, oversight, public service, trust, participation, and network adaptation.

  1. Opportunistic capture.
    Each active official moves toward the most rewarding and least-watched reachable node. The official defects when the expected gain, weighted by opportunism, exceeds the expected punishment, weighted by risk aversion and local detection risk. This gives operational form to cost comparison (Chabanna, 2019). Detection risk falls when more officials already defect at the same node, creating a “safety in numbers” effect based on arrest-probability rule (Epstein, 2002).

  2. Complicity ties and peer trust.
    Corrupt exchange requires partners. Officials therefore hold weighted complicity ties. A corrupt route is usable only when mutual peer trust is high enough. Oversight can therefore damage not only individual actors, but also the ties that make collusion possible. The network forms through preferential attachment: new officials tend to connect to already well-connected officials. This produces a skewed structure with a few high-degree hubs (Kovács & Yorke-Smith, 2025; Karnowski & Szufel, 2025).

  3. Adaptive opportunism.
    Officials do not have fixed dispositions. Their willingness to defect rises after undetected capture and falls after being caught. A caught official may also inform on a partner and cut a tie. In this way, enforcement can fragment the network from within (Duru & Cochran, 2022).

  1. Patrons as insulated hubs.
    A well-connected client can become a patron. Patrons do not access resource nodes directly. They collect a share of what their clients capture, shield those clients from detection, and cannot be caught themselves. Power here is modeled as network position rather than formal office (Fisun, 2016; Huss, 2023). The number of patrons is capped, and a patron who stops earning is demoted.

  2. Two-channel oversight.
    Oversight is split into disclosure and enforcement. Disclosure is bottom-up: watchdog monitoring, combined with what audits reveal, determines what citizens can see. Enforcement is top-down: state audit creates the punishment pressure that officials take into account. Keeping these channels separate makes it possible to model a familiar but politically important regime: everyone sees corruption, but nobody is punished. This is a sharp version of social trap (Bader et al., 2019; Rothstein, 2005).

  1. Network adaptation under pressure.
    When detection makes a node unprofitable, the network adapts. The model distinguishes three responses. A caught but solvent official relocates to a safer node. Overstaffed or unfunded offices shed people. Informing splits the network into smaller cells. These are treated as relocation, dissolution, and fragmentation: three forms of adaptation through which patronage can persist under pressure (Fisun, 2016, 2023).

  2. Trust update and cascade.
    Citizens revise trust from three sources. They respond to the quality of nearby institutions (Rothstein, 2005), to the assessments of nearby citizens whose views are close enough to matter, following bounded confidence (Gestefeld & Lorenz, 2023), and to local cascade pressure once enough neighbors have already shifted into distrust (Watts, 2002). Two asymmetries matter. Trust erodes faster than it recovers, and distrust has hysteresis: a citizen does not leave distrust at the same point where they entered it, but only after trust rises sufficiently above that level (Giardini & Vilone, 2021).

  3. Participation channel.
    Trust shapes participation; participation drives watchdog activity; watchdog activity raises disclosure; and disclosure, together with enforcement, raises detection. The sign of the first link is controlled by the user. If the sign is positive, distrust mobilizes participation. If it is negative, distrust demobilizes citizens (Johnson & Melnikov, 2009; Rothstein, 2005). The mechanism also draws on the slippery-slope framework: enforcement that has lost legitimacy buys less compliance for each additional unit of pressure (Kirchler et al., 2008).

  1. Closed, conserved society with three states.
    The model population is fixed. No one is created or removed after setup. Every person belongs to one of three groups: the public, officials, or watchdogs. Officials and watchdogs can be active or in reserve. Recruiting, redeploying, laying off, and demobilizing move people between these states, but the total population stays constant. Dissatisfaction and understaffing recruit officials; local distrust recruits watchdogs.

  1. Recruitment disposition, or the rotten barrel.
    A new official’s starting disposition is the geometric mean of two things: the cynicism they bring from the public, and the corruption norm of the office they enter. A corrupt office therefore corrupts new arrivals. This places social trap at the point of recruitment, not only at the level of the whole system (Rothstein, 2005).

  2. Service delivery with leakage.
    Corrupt officials deliver less public value than honest ones. Lower service quality reduces citizen satisfaction, and satisfaction feeds into trust. This is a modeling choice in the long “grease versus sand” debate over corruption: whether corruption sometimes helps systems work around blockages, or whether it mainly obstructs delivery (Kaufmann & Wei, 1999; Lui, 1985). This model takes the skeptical side.

  3. Funding, conditionality, wartime shock, and adaptive audit.
    Sponsors fund a pool distributed across resource nodes. An optional conditionality rule cuts funding when exposure or distrust becomes high. A wartime surge periodically increases money while relaxing scrutiny. This prevents the model from settling into a single stable risk pattern. The audited set also adapts: it drops the least relevant audited node and moves toward the most recently exposed high-risk node. The result is a slow chase between oversight and corruption. The sponsor and conditionality rules are stylized assumptions and should be read as such.

3. How it works together, and how to read the interface

One tick runs the whole system once.

At the beginning of the tick, sponsors allocate funding across resource nodes. This allocation is adjusted by wartime surge and, if enabled, by conditionality. Disclosure and enforcement are then calculated for each node from the audited set and from watchdog coverage. Active officials decide whether to capture resources, while patrons collect their share from clients.

The complicity network then reacts. Caught officials pay fines and may inform on partners. Some officials relocate to safer nodes. Offices adjust staffing when they become overstaffed or underfunded. Oversight also moves: audits shift toward recent exposure, while watchdogs move toward places where distrust is deepest.

Citizens update trust after these institutional and network reactions. They respond to service quality, to visible corruption, and to nearby citizens. Some cross the threshold into distrust, and local flips can become a cascade. Trust then shapes participation. Participation affects watchdog activity. The population is redistributed between the public, officials, watchdogs, and their reserve states. The tick closes when current trust and exposure shape the next round of funding, oversight, and recruitment.

Controls. SETUP builds the society, and GO runs the model. The other controls define the regime and the pace of change.

  • scenario sets the overall regime. baseline is a moderate setting with mild surges. weak-state is a captured, low-capacity setting where corruption is disclosed but rarely punished. high-shock produces frequent and severe surges.
  • threshold-mean sets how much revealed corruption citizens tolerate before trust drops. Lower values make trust easier to tip.
  • erosion-rate sets how quickly trust falls under exposure.
  • watched-share sets the fraction of nodes under top-down audit.
  • watched-ramp-rate gradually increases or decreases the watched share during a run.
  • tie-usability sets how easily complicity ties can be used for collusion.
  • trust-participation-slope sets the contested link between trust and participation. Positive values mean distrust mobilizes; negative values mean it demobilizes.
  • conditionality? turns sponsor funding cuts under high exposure or distrust on or off.

The view. The world is arranged by role. Citizens are at the bottom. Watchdogs sit above them. Resource nodes cross the middle. Clients sit above their nodes. Patrons are placed at the top. The two reserves sit on the edges: official reserve on the left, watchdog reserve on the right. Color and size show the current state of each actor.

Resource nodes are sky blue when audited. Otherwise, they are shaded green by service quality: dim when service is poor, bright when service is good. Larger nodes have stronger enforcement pressure.

Officials are red when they captured resources in the current tick, yellow when caught, and gray when they complied. Patrons are violet and larger. Reserve officials are small and gray.

Watchdogs are orange, with size showing monitoring activity. Reserve watchdogs are small and dim.

Citizens are blue when trusting, with darker blue showing higher trust. They turn red and grow larger when they become distrustful.

Monitors. The monitors are grouped by what they report. Trust is tracked through mean trust, the share of distrustful citizens, satisfaction, and the largest cascade so far. Corruption is tracked through corrupt share, opaque share, and exposure rate. Oversight is tracked through disclosure and enforcement. Participation shows the feedback channel between citizens and watchdogs.

The complicity network is tracked through density and modularity. Density shows how many ties remain. Modularity shows how strongly the network is divided into separate cells. The population monitors show active officials, official reserve, active patrons, active watchdogs, watchdog reserve, and the public. Two additional monitors check the system itself: the funding pool and CONSERVED?, which confirms that the population count never changes.

Plots. Four plots summarize the dynamics.

Institutional trust shows mean trust, the share of distrustful citizens, and satisfaction.

Corruption and oversight shows corrupt share, opaque share, exposure, disclosure, and enforcement on the same axis. This makes the gap between visibility and punishment easy to see.

Complicity network shows density and modularity. Dissolution appears as falling density. Fragmentation appears as rising modularity. Relocation appears when corrupt flow moves to opaque nodes while density remains stable.

Population (closed society) shows active officials, official reserve, active watchdogs, watchdog reserve, and the public scaled down tenfold. This plot shows the society “breathing” as people move between public, official, watchdog, and reserve positions.

4. Experiments and things to try

The model includes four BehaviorSpace experiments. Each experiment crosses a slider sweep with the three scenarios.

  • E1, trust tipping (RQ1) sweeps threshold-mean and erosion-rate. It reports the perceived-corruption level at which the flip rate accelerates, the distribution of cascade sizes, and whether trust settles or locks into a low level (Watts, 2002).

  • E2, transparency and placement (RQ2) sweeps watched-share against tie-usability. It shows whether the corrupt network dissolves, fragments, or relocates.

  • E3, participation sign sweeps trust-participation-slope from clearly negative to clearly positive. It marks the boundary between a self-reinforcing trap and a balancing regime (Rothstein, 2005; Bader et al., 2019).

  • E4, reversibility ramps watched-share up in one run and down in another, with conditionality on and off. It measures the width of the hysteresis loop and the time needed for recovery.

A few additional moves show the model’s range.

Compare the three scenarios. Baseline is expected to oscillate with the shock schedule. Weak-state is expected to slide into a low-trust, high-corruption trap and remain there. High-shock is expected to swing hardest.

Watch the disclosure-enforcement gap. In weak-state, disclosure should rise while enforcement remains weak. Citizens see the theft, but no one is punished. That visibility without sanction can still destroy trust.

Ramp scrutiny up during a run. Raise watched-ramp-rate and observe whether corrupt flow disappears or moves elsewhere. The key indicators are opaque share and network density.

Flip the participation sign. Move trust-participation-slope from negative to positive. This switches the model between a trap, where distrust weakens civic action, and a self-correcting regime, where distrust mobilizes oversight.

Move public tolerance. Vary threshold-mean to shift between a forgiving public and a strict public. This helps locate the tipping band.

Toggle conditionality. Turn conditionality? on and off to see whether cutting funding under exposure helps contain corruption or instead deepens the trap.

Watch conservation hold. As active and reserve tiers rise and fall, CONSERVED? should remain true. The model redistributes people; it does not create or lose them.

The behaviors above are design intentions of an uncalibrated model. They can show that a set of mechanisms is sufficient to produce a pattern. They cannot, by themselves, show that these mechanisms operate in the world.

Three steps would make the model testable rather than only demonstrative. First, it could be fitted to procurement data from Prozorro and to trust-survey series from the Razumkov Centre and the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology. Second, the single trust value could be replaced with a per-institution trust vector, allowing the model to reproduce the Ukrainian asymmetry between high trust in the army and low trust in courts and prosecutors. Third, disclosure and enforcement could be moved out of scenario presets and onto separate sliders, so that the transparency experiment can directly vary the balance between corruption being seen and corruption being punished.
